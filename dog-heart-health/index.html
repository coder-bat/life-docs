Understanding Canine Heart Health

Heart disease affects approximately 10% of all dogs and is a leading cause of death in senior dogs. The two most common forms are:

  • Chronic Valvular Disease (CVD): Affects 75% of dogs with heart disease, primarily small breeds
  • Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM): Affects large and giant breeds, accounts for 15% of cases

Early detection and intervention can significantly extend quality of life and longevity in dogs with heart conditions.

Normal Heart Rates & Cardiac Parameters

1. Resting Heart Rate by Size

Dog Size Weight Range Resting Heart Rate Average
Large Dogs >25 kg (55 lbs) 60-100 bpm 70-80 bpm
Medium Dogs 10-25 kg (22-55 lbs) 70-120 bpm 90-100 bpm
Small Dogs <10 kg (22 lbs) 90-140 bpm 100-120 bpm
Puppies (0-12 months) All sizes 120-160 bpm 140 bpm
Senior Dogs (7+ years) All sizes Same as adult May be 5-10 bpm lower

2. Breed-Specific Heart Rate Norms

Cavalier King Charles Spaniel

Normal Resting: 90-130 bpm

Concerning: Consistently >150 bpm or <70 bpm

Monitor closely due to extremely high MVD risk (up to 100% by age 10)

Doberman Pinscher

Normal Resting: 60-90 bpm

Concerning: <50 bpm or >110 bpm at rest

50-60% develop DCM by age 8-10; 30% die suddenly

Boxer

Normal Resting: 70-110 bpm

Monitor For: Irregular rhythm, syncope

30-40% affected by ARVC; Holter monitoring essential

Great Dane

Normal Resting: 60-90 bpm

DCM Risk: 5-10% of population

Annual echocardiography recommended from age 2-3

3. Cardiac Biomarkers (Blood Tests)

NT-proBNP (Natriuretic Peptide)

  • Normal: <900 pmol/L
  • Elevated: 900-1,800 pmol/L (suggests occult heart disease)
  • Significantly Elevated: >1,800 pmol/L (likely heart disease)
  • Heart Failure: Usually >2,000-3,000 pmol/L

Cardiac Troponin I

  • Normal: <0.06 ng/mL
  • Elevated: 0.06-0.2 ng/mL
  • Myocardial Injury: >0.2 ng/mL
  • Indicates acute cardiac muscle damage

Blood Pressure

  • Normal Systolic: 110-160 mmHg
  • Optimal: 120-140 mmHg
  • Hypertension: >160 mmHg (moderate), >180 mmHg (severe)
  • Hypotension: <90 mmHg

4. Exercise Heart Rate Response

Normal Response During Exercise:

  • Moderate Exercise (Brisk Walk): 1.5-2x resting rate
  • Vigorous Exercise (Running, Playing): 2-3x resting rate
  • Maximum in Healthy Adults: 180-220 bpm
  • Athletic/Working Dogs: Can reach 260-300 bpm during intense work

Recovery Time: Heart rate should return to within 10% of resting within 5-10 minutes

⚠️ Concerning Heart Rate Findings

  • Heart rate remains >150% of baseline after 10 minutes rest
  • Irregular rhythm during or after exercise
  • Heart rate drops suddenly during exercise
  • Resting heart rate consistently >150% of breed average
  • Very low resting rate (<40 bpm) in non-athletic dogs

5. How to Check Your Dog's Heart Rate

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Have your dog lie down or stand calmly
  2. Place your hand on the left side of chest, just behind the elbow
  3. Feel for the heartbeat (or use a stethoscope)
  4. Count beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4 for beats per minute (bpm)
  5. Alternatively, count for 60 seconds for more accuracy
  6. Record the rate and note if rhythm is regular

Best Time to Check: When dog is calm and resting, not immediately after exercise or excitement

Frequency: Weekly for high-risk breeds; monthly for others; daily if cardiac disease diagnosed

6. Echocardiographic Reference Values

Key Measurements (vary by breed and size):

  • Ejection Fraction (EF): Normal 60-80%; DCM typically <40%
  • Fractional Shortening (FS): Normal 25-45%; DCM usually <20%
  • Left Atrial to Aortic Ratio (LA:Ao): Normal <1.5; Stage B2 MVD >1.6

These measurements can only be obtained through echocardiography performed by a veterinarian or veterinary cardiologist.

Optimal Nutrition for Heart Health

1. Grain-Inclusive Diets & DCM Risk

Recent FDA investigations (2018-2022) found a correlation between grain-free diets and Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM), particularly in diets high in peas, lentils, and potatoes.

  • Recommendation: Feed grain-inclusive diets from WSAVA-compliant manufacturers (Royal Canin, Hill's, Purina, Iams, Eukanuba)
  • Ensure diets meet AAFCO (Association of American Feed Control Officials) standards
  • Consult board-certified veterinary nutritionists for custom diets

2. Essential Nutrients for Cardiac Function

Taurine

Essential amino acid for cardiac muscle function. Deficiency linked to DCM in Golden Retrievers and other breeds.

Sources: Animal-based proteins (chicken, turkey, fish)

L-Carnitine

Supports energy metabolism in heart muscle cells. Deficiency associated with DCM.

Sources: Red meat, dairy products

Omega-3 Fatty Acids

EPA and DHA reduce inflammation and support cardiovascular health.

Sources: Fish oil, salmon, sardines

Magnesium

Regulates heart rhythm and supports muscle contraction.

Sources: Whole grains, leafy vegetables

3. Sodium Restriction for Dogs with Heart Disease

For dogs diagnosed with heart failure, moderate sodium restriction (0.08-0.12% dry matter basis) helps reduce fluid retention. However, severe restriction is not recommended without veterinary guidance.

4. Adequate Protein Intake

Maintain high-quality protein levels (minimum 25-30% for adult dogs) to prevent cardiac cachexia (muscle wasting) common in heart disease.

Exercise & Physical Activity

Benefits of Regular Exercise

  • Maintains healthy body weight (obesity increases cardiac workload)
  • Improves cardiovascular efficiency and endurance
  • Reduces blood pressure and inflammation
  • Enhances overall quality of life

Exercise Guidelines by Life Stage

Life Stage Duration Frequency Recommendations
Puppies (0-1 year) 15-30 minutes 2-3 times daily Short, gentle play sessions; avoid overexertion during growth
Adult Dogs (1-7 years) 30-60 minutes Daily Brisk walks, running, swimming, interactive play
Senior Dogs (7+ years) 20-40 minutes Daily Low-impact activities, gentle walks, swimming
Dogs with Heart Disease 10-20 minutes As tolerated Gentle walks, monitored activity; avoid extreme temperatures

⚠️ Warning Signs During Exercise

Stop exercise immediately if your dog shows:

  • Excessive panting or difficulty breathing
  • Bluish gums or tongue (cyanosis)
  • Weakness, collapse, or reluctance to continue
  • Persistent coughing

Weight Management

Maintaining ideal body weight is crucial. Obesity increases heart disease risk by 40% according to veterinary studies. Use body condition scoring (BCS) to monitor your dog's weight.

Evidence-Based Supplements

Always consult with a veterinarian before starting any supplements. These should complement, not replace, veterinary care.

Supplements with Strong Evidence

1. Omega-3 Fatty Acids (Fish Oil)

Evidence Level: High

Benefits:

  • Reduces cardiac arrhythmias
  • Decreases inflammation and cytokine production
  • May slow progression of heart failure
  • Improves cachexia (muscle wasting) in heart disease

Dosage: 50-100 mg/kg of combined EPA+DHA daily (veterinarian-guided)

Studies: Multiple peer-reviewed studies in Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine demonstrate benefits

2. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)

Evidence Level: Moderate to High

Benefits:

  • Powerful antioxidant protecting cardiac cells
  • Improves energy production in heart muscle
  • May reduce progression of DCM
  • Helps dogs on certain heart medications

Dosage: 30-90 mg twice daily for medium-sized dogs (adjust by weight)

Note: Particularly beneficial for Boxers with cardiomyopathy

3. Taurine Supplementation

Evidence Level: High (for deficiency-related DCM)

Benefits:

  • Essential for cardiac muscle contractility
  • Reverses DCM when deficiency is the cause
  • Particularly important for Golden Retrievers, American Cocker Spaniels

Dosage: 500-1000 mg twice daily for large dogs (veterinarian-guided)

Testing: Blood taurine levels should be measured before supplementation

4. L-Carnitine

Evidence Level: Moderate

Benefits:

  • Supports cardiac muscle metabolism
  • May benefit Boxers and Doberman Pinschers with DCM
  • Aids in fat metabolism

Dosage: 50-100 mg/kg three times daily

Supplements with Limited Evidence

  • Hawthorn extract: Some evidence in human medicine; limited canine studies
  • Vitamin E: Antioxidant properties; benefits unclear in dogs
  • Selenium: May support cardiac function; use cautiously due to toxicity risk

Preventive Care & Early Detection

1. Regular Veterinary Examinations

  • Annual exams for dogs under 7 years
  • Bi-annual exams for senior dogs (7+ years)
  • Cardiac auscultation (listening to heart) at every visit
  • Blood pressure monitoring for at-risk breeds

2. Diagnostic Screening

Recommended Screening Tests:

  • Echocardiography: Ultrasound of the heart; gold standard for detecting structural abnormalities
  • Pro-BNP Blood Test: Biomarker for heart disease; useful for screening asymptomatic dogs
  • ECG (Electrocardiogram): Detects arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities
  • Chest X-rays: Evaluates heart size and pulmonary congestion

High-Risk Breeds Should Start Screening at:

  • Doberman Pinschers: 2 years old
  • Boxers: 1-2 years old
  • Cavalier King Charles Spaniels: 1 year old
  • Great Danes, Irish Wolfhounds: 2-3 years old

3. Dental Health

Critical Connection: Periodontal disease increases risk of endocarditis (heart infection) by up to 6 times.

  • Daily tooth brushing with veterinary toothpaste
  • Professional dental cleanings as recommended
  • Dental chews approved by Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC)

4. Parasite Prevention

Heartworm Prevention: Heartworm disease directly damages the heart and pulmonary arteries.

  • Year-round monthly heartworm preventive medication
  • Annual heartworm testing
  • Mosquito control in living areas

5. Environmental Factors

  • Avoid secondhand smoke: Increases cardiovascular disease risk in pets
  • Minimize stress: Chronic stress elevates cortisol and blood pressure
  • Maintain comfortable temperature: Extreme heat or cold stresses the cardiovascular system

Warning Signs of Heart Disease

🚨 Seek Immediate Veterinary Care If You Notice:

  • Persistent coughing, especially at night or when lying down
  • Difficulty breathing or rapid breathing (more than 40 breaths per minute at rest)
  • Exercise intolerance or reluctance to walk/play
  • Fainting or collapse episodes (syncope)
  • Bluish gums or tongue (indicates poor oxygenation)
  • Abdominal distension (fluid accumulation - ascites)
  • Restlessness at night, difficulty finding comfortable position
  • Loss of appetite or weight loss despite normal eating
  • Weakness or lethargy

Early Signs (Often Subtle)

  • Decreased stamina during walks
  • Occasional cough, especially after excitement
  • Slightly increased breathing rate at rest
  • Reduced interest in activities
  • Heart murmur detected during routine exam

Monitoring at Home

Resting Respiratory Rate (RRR)

Count breaths for 60 seconds while your dog is sleeping/resting:

  • Normal: 10-30 breaths per minute
  • Concerning: Consistently above 35-40 breaths per minute
  • Action: Contact veterinarian if elevated for 2+ days

How to Count Breaths

  1. Watch chest rise and fall while dog is asleep
  2. One breath = one complete rise and fall
  3. Count for 60 seconds
  4. Record daily and track trends

Breeds at Higher Risk

Chronic Valvular Disease (CVD) - Small Breeds

Cavalier King Charles Spaniel

Risk: Extremely High - up to 100% by age 10 (30% by age 5, 70% by age 9)

Normal Heart Rate: 90-130 bpm

Early-onset mitral valve disease; screening recommended from age 1; LA:Ao ratio >1.6 indicates Stage B2

Dachshund

Risk: High - 35-40% by age 10

Normal Heart Rate: 90-130 bpm

Prone to mitral valve degeneration; 60% have endocardiosis by age 13+

Chihuahua

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 100-140 bpm

Mitral valve disease common in older dogs; also predisposed to patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

Miniature Poodle

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 90-120 bpm

Valvular disease typically after age 8

Cocker Spaniel

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 70-120 bpm

Both mitral valve disease and DCM reported; taurine levels should be monitored

Yorkshire Terrier

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 100-140 bpm

Small breed predisposition to valve disease

Dilated Cardiomyopathy (DCM) - Large Breeds

Doberman Pinscher

Risk: Very High - 50-60% affected by age 8-10

Normal Heart Rate: 60-90 bpm; <50 or >110 bpm concerning

Sudden death in 30% without prior symptoms; genetic testing available (PDK4 gene); screen from age 2 with annual echo + Holter

Boxer

Risk: Very High - 30-40% in some lineages

Normal Heart Rate: 70-110 bpm

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC); >300 VPCs/24 hours abnormal; CoQ10 may help; 25-30% sudden death risk

Great Dane

Risk: High - 5-10% DCM prevalence

Normal Heart Rate: 60-90 bpm

DCM common; taurine deficiency sometimes implicated; screen from age 2-3

Irish Wolfhound

Risk: Very High - 20-24% develop DCM by age 5

Normal Heart Rate: 60-100 bpm

Atrial fibrillation common; mean lifespan only 6-8 years partly due to heart disease

Golden Retriever

Risk: Moderate to High - 2-5% taurine-deficient DCM

Normal Heart Rate: 70-110 bpm

DCM associated with taurine deficiency; 75-90% improve with supplementation; blood taurine should be >250 nmol/mL (whole blood)

Saint Bernard

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 60-90 bpm

Giant breed susceptibility to DCM

Newfoundland

Risk: High

Normal Heart Rate: 60-90 bpm

Subvalvular aortic stenosis (SAS) also common; screening from age 1 recommended; pressure gradient >50 mmHg disqualifies from breeding

Portuguese Water Dog

Risk: Moderate

Normal Heart Rate: 70-100 bpm

DCM linked to diet in some cases

Other Genetic Heart Conditions

  • Subaortic Stenosis: Newfoundlands, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds, Rottweilers
  • Pulmonic Stenosis: Bulldogs, Boxers, Beagles
  • Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA): Maltese, Poodles, German Shepherds

Genetic Testing

Some breeds have genetic tests available for heart disease risk:

  • Doberman Pinscher: PDK4 gene mutation testing for DCM
  • Boxer: Genetic screening for ARVC

Consult with breeders about health clearances and cardiac screening of parents.

Australia-Specific Cardiac Health Information

1. Heartworm Disease - Endemic in Australia

⚠️ Heartworm disease is ENDEMIC throughout Australia and poses a significant threat to canine cardiac health. Year-round prevention is MANDATORY for all dogs.

Geographic Risk Distribution

  • High-Risk Regions: Northern Queensland (5-20% prevalence in unprotected dogs), Northern Territory, coastal Queensland and NSW
  • Moderate-Risk: Southern Queensland, coastal NSW and Victoria, Perth metropolitan area
  • Lower-Risk (but NOT zero): Tasmania, inland areas
  • Year-Round Transmission: Northern Australia (tropical climate)
  • Seasonal Peak: Southern Australia (November-April)

Cardiac Impact of Heartworm:

  • Adult worms live in pulmonary arteries and right side of heart
  • Causes right-sided heart failure and pulmonary hypertension
  • Permanent cardiac damage even after treatment
  • Caval Syndrome: Severe worm burden in vena cava - 50-70% mortality even with surgery

Prevention (APVMA-Registered Products):

  • Monthly Oral/Topical: Ivermectin, milbemycin oxime, moxidectin, selamectin combinations
  • Annual Injection: Moxidectin (ProHeart SR-12) - convenient for compliance
  • Cost: A$10-30 per month vs. A$2,000-5,000+ treatment cost
  • AVA Recommendation: Year-round prevention for ALL Australian dogs

Only 40-60% of Australian dogs receive regular heartworm prevention despite 100% effectiveness when properly administered.

2. Heat Stress and Australian Climate

Extreme Heat Challenges:

  • Australian summers regularly reach 35-45°C (95-113°F) in many regions
  • Prolonged heatwaves (multiple days >38°C/100°F) common
  • High humidity in coastal and tropical areas increases heat stress
  • Urban heat islands make cities 5-10°C warmer than surrounding areas

Cardiac Implications:

  • Increased cardiac workload for thermoregulation
  • Dehydration affects cardiac output and blood pressure
  • Dogs with heart disease particularly vulnerable to heat
  • Heat stroke can cause acute cardiac injury (elevated troponin)

High-Risk Breeds in Australian Climate

  • Brachycephalic Breeds: Bulldogs, Pugs, French Bulldogs (poor heat tolerance)
  • Large/Giant Breeds: Great Danes, Saint Bernards (higher metabolic heat)
  • Dark-Coated Dogs: Absorb more radiant heat
  • Obese Dogs: Reduced heat dissipation
  • Dogs with Heart Disease: Limited cardiac reserve for heat stress

Australian Summer Exercise Guidelines:

  • Safe Times: Early morning only (<8 AM) or late evening (>7 PM)
  • AVOID: 10 AM - 5 PM during heat (particularly November-March)
  • Pavement Temperature: Can exceed 50°C (122°F) - burns paws and increases body temperature
  • Air Conditioning: Essential for brachycephalic and cardiac patients during heatwaves
  • Cooling Strategies: Wet towels, paddling pools, cooling mats, access to shade

3. Tick Paralysis - Australian Endemic Parasite

Paralysis Tick (Ixodes holocyclus):

  • Distribution: Coastal Queensland and NSW; limited eastern Victoria
  • Peak Season: September-March
  • Cardiac Effects: Tick toxin causes respiratory paralysis → hypoxia → cardiac stress
  • Arrhythmias: Reported in severe cases
  • Dogs with Heart Disease: Poorly tolerate respiratory compromise - higher mortality risk

Prevention: Essential year-round in endemic areas (Bravecto, NexGard, Simparica, Advantix)
